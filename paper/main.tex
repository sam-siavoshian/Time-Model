\begin{abstract}
Large language models can reason about time when time is written in the prompt, but the standard transformer interface gives them no first-class input for elapsed wall-clock duration. This limits interactive and agentic systems in which the same visible text may require different responses depending on whether seconds, hours, or days have passed. This paper studies whether a frozen autoregressive language model can be conditioned on a continuous scalar elapsed-time signal without updating its base weights. We introduce \emph{chronometric injection} (\CI), a residual-stream conditioning channel that encodes elapsed seconds $\Tau$ with sinusoidal and logarithmic features and injects that encoding into Qwen 2.5 3B through zero-initialized AdaLN-style FiLM projectors. The novelty is not FiLM, LoRA, or sinusoidal time encoding individually; it is the causal and mechanistic characterization of an elapsed-time channel inside an otherwise frozen language model. Linear probes show that the channel is transmitted mechanically: recoverability of $\log \Tau$ jumps from chance at layer 0 ($R^2=-0.005$) to near-perfect at layer 1 ($R^2=0.99990$), remains above $0.9995$ through the network, and disappears when the FiLM gates are zeroed. A CLOCK-heldout replication preserves this layer-1 axis ($R^2=0.99984\pm0.00007$), showing that the representation is not merely induced by duration-string supervision. Causal ablations further show that LoRA alone cannot explain the results, that sign-flipping the learned gates reverses or disrupts parsed time behavior, and that the pathway is distributed with mid-depth dominance. Behavioral results are narrower: simple clock-readout tests are best treated as supervised wiring checks, prompt-injected time beats \CI{} on direct recall tasks, and the pre-registered TPDR v2 benchmark yields a corrected response-shape effect on deliberative wording for the headline seed pair. An identical-prompt Temporal Policy Switching benchmark, in which only the hidden $\Tau$ scalar varies, returns a negative behavioral result: \CI{} v15s reaches hidden-only policy accuracy $0.344\pm0.005$ across three seeds (vanilla 0.349), and refresh probability does not rise monotonically with $\log\Tau$ (mean $r=-0.270$), confirming that the channel is transmitted but not, in the zero-shot setting tested here, used by the output policy. Overall, \CI{} is best understood as a traceable residual-stream time-conditioning channel, not as evidence of subjective time perception and not as a universal replacement for prompt-based timestamps.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Modern language models contain extensive textual knowledge about time. They can define a minute, compute a deadline, describe weekdays, and answer questions about dates when those facts appear in the context. This ability should not be confused with an internal signal for elapsed wall-clock duration. In a standard decoder-only transformer, the native sequence signal is token position, not real elapsed time.

This distinction matters for interactive and agentic systems. A user may leave a session for ten seconds or ten days, but the model receives only the next sequence of tokens unless an external system writes a timestamp into the prompt. Prompt timestamps are useful, but they remain discrete text tokens that the model must attend to and interpret at generation time. They do not provide a continuous input channel that can modulate hidden states independently of the visible prompt.

The technical challenge is therefore not to teach the model the word ``time.'' The challenge is to give a frozen pretrained model a continuous scalar input $\Tau$ and then determine whether that scalar enters the residual stream, whether the model causally uses it, and whether the resulting behavior is anything more than supervised template recall. A credible answer must separate three issues: mechanical transmission of the signal, causal use of the signal by the model, and downstream behavioral generalization.

Prior work provides pieces of this problem but not the full object studied here. Positional encodings and continuous-time representations show that scalar or sequential time can be embedded into vectors. FiLM and adaptive normalization show that side information can modulate hidden states. LoRA and related parameter-efficient adaptation methods show that pretrained models can be modified without full fine-tuning. Mechanistic interpretability shows that structured variables can sometimes be recovered from hidden states. However, these lines do not by themselves establish whether externally supplied elapsed wall-clock time can be injected into a frozen LLM as a causal residual-stream channel and then isolated through interventions.

This paper introduces \emph{chronometric injection} (\CI), a controlled study of elapsed-time conditioning in a frozen LLM. \CI{} maps real elapsed seconds $\Tau$ to a fixed 31-dimensional encoding $\chi(\Tau)$ and applies per-layer AdaLN-Zero FiLM modulation to the residual stream of Qwen 2.5 3B. The base model is frozen; trainable parameters are confined to the chrono projectors and, in the main configuration, a small LoRA surface used to let the model act on the modulated states.

The central claim is deliberately narrow. \CI{} establishes a causal residual-stream channel for continuous elapsed time. It does not establish subjective time perception, it does not prove that the model autonomously tracks time during periods without input, and it does not show that residual injection is always better than prompt injection. The strongest evidence is mechanistic: the channel is linearly recoverable after one transformer block, disappears when the gate is disabled, survives removal of clock-readout supervision, and changes behavior under sign-flip interventions.

The paper makes four contributions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item It formulates elapsed-time conditioning for a frozen autoregressive LLM as an input-channel problem rather than as a prompt-formatting problem.
  \item It implements a simple chronometric channel using a sinusoidal-plus-log encoding of $\Tau$ and AdaLN-Zero FiLM modulation of the residual stream.
  \item It provides causal evidence that the channel is transmitted and used: probe recovery, zero-gate controls, LoRA-only collapse, sign-flip interventions, and held-out-supervision tests.
  \item It narrows the behavioral claim by showing where prompt injection is stronger, where \CI{} only produces exploratory response-shape evidence, and why the in-house clock-readout tasks should be interpreted as wiring checks rather than evidence of time perception.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section~II summarizes related work. Section~III defines the problem and scope. Section~IV describes the chronometric channel. Section~V gives the evaluation setup. Section~VI reports results. Section~VII discusses implications and limitations. Section~VIII concludes with future work.

\section{Related Work}

The present work intersects four bodies of prior work: temporal representations in sequence models, feature-wise conditioning, parameter-efficient adaptation, and mechanistic analysis of language-model representations. These areas provide the technical background for \CI{}, but none directly answers the causal question studied here: whether an externally supplied continuous elapsed-time scalar can become a traceable and intervention-sensitive hidden-state channel in a frozen autoregressive language model.

\subsection{Temporal information in sequence models}

Transformers encode order through positional representations, but token order is not equivalent to wall-clock time. Standard positional encodings describe where a token occurs in a sequence, whereas elapsed duration is an external scalar that may change independently of token count. This distinction is important because two identical prompts can correspond to different real-world states if different amounts of time have passed between turns.

Continuous-time representations such as Time2Vec show that scalar time can be mapped into vector features with periodic and nonperiodic components~\cite{vaswani2017attention,kazemi2019time2vec}. These encodings are useful background for representing $\Tau$, but they do not specify how a frozen LLM should receive or use the resulting vector. \CI{} adopts a simple sinusoidal-plus-log encoding, but studies the separate question of whether that encoding becomes causally available inside the residual stream.

\subsection{Feature-wise conditioning and residual modulation}

Feature-wise linear modulation (FiLM) conditions a network by applying learned feature-wise transformations to intermediate activations~\cite{perez2018film}. Adaptive layer-normalization variants, including AdaLN-Zero-style conditioning, are widely used in diffusion transformers to insert conditioning information while preserving the initial behavior of the unconditioned model~\cite{peebles2023dit}. These mechanisms show that side information can be inserted into neural activations in a controlled way.

\CI{} uses this family of mechanisms, but the primitive itself is not claimed as new. The contribution is instead the causal characterization of what happens when such modulation carries elapsed wall-clock seconds into a frozen language model. The relevant question is not whether FiLM can condition a network in general, but whether the resulting elapsed-time pathway can be isolated by probes, zero-gate controls, held-out-supervision tests, and sign-flip interventions.

\subsection{Parameter-efficient adaptation}

Low-rank adaptation (LoRA) and IA3 are parameter-efficient methods for adapting pretrained language models without updating all base weights~\cite{hu2021lora,liu2022ia3}. These methods reduce the cost of adaptation and make it possible to study modifications that leave the pretrained model largely intact. They are therefore natural tools for studying controlled interventions in frozen or mostly frozen LLMs.

In \CI{}, LoRA is not the hypothesized source of elapsed-time information. It is a small trainable action surface that can help the model use the chrono-modulated states. The ablations below test this distinction directly by freezing the chrono gates while leaving LoRA trainable, freezing LoRA while training the chrono pathway, and substituting IA3. These controls are necessary because otherwise an observed behavior could be mistakenly attributed to the chrono channel when it is actually caused by the adapter.

\subsection{Mechanistic analysis of representations}

Mechanistic work has shown that language models can contain linearly recoverable representations of structured concepts, including spatial and temporal variables~\cite{gurnee2023language}. Such work motivates the use of probes and intervention-based analysis rather than only end-task accuracy. However, probing an unmodified model and injecting a new continuous channel are different questions.

\CI{} studies the complementary intervention problem. Instead of asking only whether a pretrained model already represents time, it asks whether a continuous elapsed-time signal can be externally supplied, become recoverable inside hidden states, and causally alter behavior under targeted interventions. This makes the paper a study of a controllable input pathway rather than a claim about naturally emergent temporal cognition.

\subsection{Synthesis and gap}

Taken together, prior work supplies useful components: temporal encodings supply features, FiLM supplies a modulation mechanism, PEFT supplies a lightweight adaptation surface, and mechanistic interpretability supplies tools for analyzing hidden states. The missing piece is a controlled study that joins these components to test a specific causal hypothesis about elapsed wall-clock conditioning in frozen LLMs. \CI{} addresses this gap by treating $\Tau$ as an external scalar input, injecting it into the residual stream, and evaluating whether the resulting pathway is transmitted, necessary, causal, and behaviorally bounded.

\section{Background and Problem Definition}

Let $f_\theta$ denote a frozen autoregressive decoder-only language model with parameters $\theta$. Let $x$ denote the visible prompt tokens and let $\Tau\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq0}$ denote elapsed wall-clock seconds since a reference event, such as the start of a session. The target is a conditioned model $g(x,\Tau)$ that can use $\Tau$ while leaving $\theta$ unchanged.

The problem is not simply to output a formatted duration string. A prompt could include ``elapsed time: 3 hours,'' and a fine-tuned model could learn to copy or transform that text. The problem studied here is whether a continuous non-token scalar can be made available inside the residual stream and whether the resulting hidden-state pathway is necessary, causal, and distinguishable from prompt-only behavior.

We therefore define success through three questions. First, \emph{transmission}: can $\Tau$ be recovered from hidden states only when the chrono channel is active? Second, \emph{causality}: do interventions on the learned gates change time-conditioned behavior in the expected direction? Third, \emph{behavioral scope}: which observed behaviors reflect channel use, which reflect supervised format recall, and which are better handled by prompt-injected timestamps?

The scope is intentionally limited. \CI{} does not model physical time passing inside the transformer between forward passes. It accepts an externally supplied value of $\Tau$ at inference time. If that value is wrong or adversarial, the model can condition on the wrong time. The paper therefore studies an architectural input pathway, not autonomous temporal consciousness, persistent internal recurrence, or subjective time perception.

\section{Proposed Approach}

\subsection{Overview}

Chronometric injection adds a continuous elapsed-time side channel to a frozen LLM. The method has three stages. First, elapsed seconds $\Tau$ are encoded as a fixed vector $\chi(\Tau)$. Second, each injected transformer layer maps $\chi(\Tau)$ to feature-wise scale and shift parameters. Third, a zero-initialized gate controls how strongly the FiLM signal perturbs the residual stream.

The design principle is conservative adaptation. At initialization, the model is exactly the frozen base model. During training, only the chrono projectors, their gates, and the optional parameter-efficient surface are updated. This makes it possible to attribute changes to the new channel rather than to broad changes in the pretrained network.

\subsection{Chronometric encoder}

The chronometric encoder maps elapsed seconds into sinusoidal features at multiple scales plus a monotone logarithmic coordinate:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\chi(\Tau)=\big[&\sin(\Tau/T_1),\cos(\Tau/T_1),\ldots,\\
&\sin(\Tau/T_{15}),\cos(\Tau/T_{15}),\log(1+\Tau)\big]^\top .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The scale divisors are
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
T\in\{&2,4,8,16,32,64,128,256,512,1024,\nonumber\\
&4096,16384,65536,86400,604800\}\ \text{seconds}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The implementation uses $\sin(\Tau/T_k)$ and $\cos(\Tau/T_k)$ directly, without a $2\pi$ multiplier; therefore $T_k$ is a radian-scale divisor and the mathematical sinusoid period is $2\pi T_k$. The largest divisors include day and week scales. The $\log(1+\Tau)$ coordinate provides a nonperiodic monotone direction. The encoder has 31 output dimensions and no learned parameters.

\subsection{Residual-stream injection}

At decoder layer $\ell$, let $h_\ell\in\mathbb{R}^d$ denote the residual-stream hidden state. The chrono projector computes
\begin{align}
\gamma_\ell &= W^\gamma_\ell\chi(\Tau)+b^\gamma_\ell,\\
\beta_\ell &= W^\beta_\ell\chi(\Tau)+b^\beta_\ell,
\end{align}
and updates the residual stream as
\begin{equation}
 h'_\ell=h_\ell+\alpha_\ell\odot\left(\gamma_\ell\odot h_\ell+\beta_\ell\right),
\label{eq:film}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_\ell\in\mathbb{R}^d$ is a learned per-channel gate. The main configuration injects this channel into 35 of 36 decoder layers.

This formulation makes the elapsed-time pathway explicit. The scalar $\Tau$ is not represented as a prompt token. It enters through $\chi(\Tau)$, affects layerwise feature transformations through $(\gamma_\ell,\beta_\ell)$, and is controlled by the gate $\alpha_\ell$.

\subsection{Zero initialization}

The projectors use an AdaLN-Zero-style initialization:
\begin{equation}
\alpha_\ell=0,\quad W^\gamma_\ell=0,\quad b^\gamma_\ell=\mathbf{1},\quad W^\beta_\ell=0,\quad b^\beta_\ell=0.
\end{equation}
At step 0, (\ref{eq:film}) reduces to $h'_\ell=h_\ell$, so the base model is unchanged. However,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial h'_\ell}{\partial \alpha_\ell}\bigg|_{\text{init}}=\gamma_\ell\odot h_\ell+\beta_\ell=h_\ell,
\end{equation}
which gives the gate a nonzero gradient on the first backward pass. This initialization is important: additive variants with zero shift initialization can trap the gate at zero because the initial gate gradient vanishes.

\subsection{Trainable surface}

The main model also trains a rank-8 LoRA adapter on attention projections and the language-model head. LoRA is not assumed to encode time by itself. It supplies a small action surface over the chrono-modulated hidden states. The key ablation freezes $\alpha$ at zero while leaving LoRA trainable; this LoRA-only model fails all time-conditioned tests, showing that the adapter alone does not explain the results.

\subsection{Algorithmic summary}

The procedure is summarized in Algorithm~\ref{alg:ci}. The algorithm is intentionally simple because the paper's main claim is not a new optimization procedure. The important property is that elapsed time enters the model through a controllable non-token pathway whose gates can be zeroed, flipped, or localized by layer.

\section{Evaluation Setup}

\subsection{Evaluation questions}

The evaluation asks four questions. Q1: does the elapsed-time signal become linearly recoverable in the residual stream? Q2: is the chrono channel necessary and causal? Q3: which behavioral tests survive when specific supervised labels are removed? Q4: when compared with prompt-injected $\Tau$, does \CI{} show a behavioral advantage beyond direct time recall?

\subsection{Model and training data}

Experiments use Qwen 2.5 3B-Instruct as the frozen base model, with a single-seed 7B scale check. Training uses 18,000 synthetic conversations over three families: CLOCK, SILENT-GAP, and PHASE. CLOCK maps elapsed time to a deterministic duration string. SILENT-GAP asks whether a large delay between turns should be acknowledged. PHASE maps day/week phase to weekday or weekend greetings. These tasks are useful for channel training, but they also create a risk of supervised label recall; held-out-supervision tests address this risk.

Elapsed time values are sampled over the interval $[1\ \mathrm{s},7\ \mathrm{days}]$ unless otherwise stated. The main evaluation is therefore an in-distribution test of channel use over the training support, not a claim of extrapolation to arbitrary durations. Extrapolation outside this range is treated as a limitation rather than as a demonstrated capability.

\subsection{Baselines and controls}

The evaluation includes zero-gate controls, LoRA-only training, chrono-only training with LoRA frozen, additive gating variants, L0-only injection, IA3 substitution, prompt-injected $\Tau$ baselines, all-layer sign flips, random half-layer sign flips, and held-out-task retraining. These controls are designed to distinguish the chrono channel from the surrounding adaptation method and from prompt-format effects.

The prompt baseline is especially important. If writing the elapsed time directly into the prompt performs better on direct recall tasks, then \CI{} should not be described as a universal replacement for timestamps. Instead, residual-stream conditioning should be evaluated for cases where elapsed time should shape generation without being part of the visible text.

\subsection{Metrics}

Mechanical transmission is measured by ridge probes predicting $\log_{10}\Tau$ from last-token hidden states at each layer. Behavioral readout is measured with T1/T1b clock correlation and log-MAE, T2 acknowledgement-rate difference, T3 weekday/weekend discrimination, and T4 pairwise KL shifts across $\Tau$. External behavior is evaluated on a \texttt{tau\_sessions} benchmark and on TPDR, a set of ambiguous decision prompts whose text does not mention time. TPDR uses lexical response-shape metrics and Holm correction across metrics.

\subsection{Reproducibility details}

The reported experiments use fixed random seeds for data generation, training, and probe fitting. Main 3B results are reported across three seeds where feasible; the 7B run is treated only as a limited scale check. The base model weights remain frozen in all main experiments. Trainable parameters are restricted to chrono projectors, gates, and the specified PEFT surface. Probe models are trained separately from the language-model training objective and operate on stored last-token hidden states. All ablations are run with the same task families and evaluation scripts unless explicitly noted.

For external submission, the following implementation details should be reported in an appendix or artifact statement: optimizer, learning rate, batch size, number of training steps, warmup schedule, LoRA target modules, LoRA scaling, hardware, precision, checkpoint-selection rule, dataset-generation code, prompt templates, probe split, and all random seeds. Without these details, the paper is semantically coherent but not fully reproducible.

\section{Results}

\subsection{The chrono signal is mechanically transmitted}

The strongest result is the hidden-state probe. In the trained model, a linear probe for $\log\Tau$ is at chance at layer 0 ($R^2=-0.005$), jumps to $R^2=0.99990$ at layer 1, and remains at least $0.9995$ through the final layer. When the learned FiLM gates are zeroed, every layer returns to chance. This establishes that the recoverable time axis is produced by the chrono channel rather than by the unmodified base model.

The CLOCK-heldout probe strengthens this interpretation. When CLOCK examples are removed from training, the layer-1 probe still achieves $R^2=0.99984\pm0.00007$ across three seeds. The channel therefore does not require the model to learn a clock-readout string in order for $\Tau$ to become linearly recoverable. The time axis is a mechanical consequence of the FiLM injection and early transformer processing.

\subsection{The channel is necessary and causal}

LoRA-only training makes all pass-style behavioral checks fail across three seeds when the chrono gates are frozen at zero. This rules out the explanation that a matched LoRA adapter can infer elapsed time from prompt artifacts or training distribution leakage. Conversely, chrono-only training with LoRA frozen passes the in-house tests across three seeds, showing that LoRA is useful but not necessary.

Gate sign-flip interventions provide a stronger causal test. Flipping all learned $\alpha$ gates at inference inverts parsed time predictions with $r=-0.9998$ in the parseable T1 setting. Randomly flipping 17 of 35 injected layers yields a bimodal distribution over 100 subsets; using $|r|>0.5$ as the preserve/invert threshold, 52 preserve the signal, 18 invert it, 23 are mixed, and 7 produce nonfinite outputs. This pattern is more consistent with a distributed signed pathway than with either a single scalar axis or a single dominant layer.

Layer-norm analysis identifies a stable mid-depth band. The top-8 layers by $|\alpha|$ norm are L20--L27 across the three trained seeds. Targeted top-8 flips collapse or invert the signal in two of three seeds, while bottom-8 flips preserve the signal in all three. This supports mid-deep dominance, although the imperfect top-8 result shows that norm ranking is not a complete proxy for functional importance.

\subsection{In-house behavioral tests are mostly wiring checks}

Table~\ref{tab:mainresults} summarizes the central quantitative results. T1 and T1b pass with high correlations, but their interpretation is limited because CLOCK training explicitly teaches a mapping from continuous $\Tau$ to a finite set of duration strings. A non-LLM linear classifier on $\chi(\Tau)$ reaches $r=0.9965$ and log-MAE $0.0267$, tighter than \CI{} on T1b. These tests therefore show that the model can use the channel; they do not prove richer time understanding.

T2 and T4 provide more meaningful channel-use evidence, especially under held-out supervision. When CLOCK supervision is removed, T1 and T1b fail because the model has no learned duration-string format, but T2 and T4 pass in all three seeds. This indicates that the chrono channel still carries usable elapsed-time information to downstream behaviors that were trained through non-CLOCK tasks.

\subsection{Surrounding architectural choices are interchangeable}

Several ablations narrow the architectural claim. L0-only injection matches four of five in-house tests, although with worse precision on T1b log-MAE and weaker T4 shifts. Additive injection with zero shift initialization fails because the gate gradient is initially zero, but additive injection with nonzero $\beta$ bias passes two of three seeds. IA3 substituted for LoRA passes with the chrono channel active and collapses when the chrono channel is frozen. These results imply that the key contribution is not a particular PEFT method or a particular per-layer placement; it is the existence of a trainable continuous elapsed-time path with a nonzero initial gate gradient.

\subsection{Prompt-injected time is stronger for direct recall}

The prompt baseline is a critical negative result. On external \texttt{tau\_sessions}, prompt-injected $\Tau$ wins on staleness accuracy, duration recall, adaptive length correlation, and the composite score. In the current artifact, \CI{} has lower composite score than prompt injection (0.377 versus 0.520), lower staleness accuracy (0.23 versus 0.51), worse duration-recall log-MAE (0.098 versus 0.064), and a negative adaptive length correlation ($r=-0.050$ versus prompt $r=+0.115$). This supports a narrower view: prompt injection is preferable when the task is to read and report time, while residual injection remains valuable mainly as a mechanistically traceable non-token channel.

\subsection{TPDR v2 is exploratory and seed-pair dependent}

TPDR tests ambiguous decision prompts whose text does not mention time. Each scenario is evaluated across several values of $\Tau$ for vanilla, prompt-injected, and \CI{} adapters. The canonical TPDR result is the pre-registered v2 replication over 200 scenarios. For the headline seed pair $(0,0)$, the primary endpoint confirms: the paired test on per-scenario rank correlation of \texttt{deliberative\_score} against $\log\Tau$, \CI{} minus Prompt, gives $t=-5.167$ on $df=45$ with one-tailed $p=2.633\times10^{-6}$ at $\alpha=0.005$, $n_{\text{paired}}=46$, and mean difference $-0.4480$ with 95\% CI $[-0.6227,-0.2734]$.

Secondary TPDR v2 endpoints are mixed rather than broad confirmation. Urgency and conditional-clause scores survive Holm correction in the secondary table, while length, hedging, and imperative count do not. Cross-seed TPDR is not stable: the pre-registered headline pair confirms, seed pair $(1,1)$ is null, and seed pair $(2,2)$ moves in the opposite direction. TPDR should therefore be read as a seed-pair-dependent response-shape finding, not as general behavioral proof that \CI{} is superior to prompt timestamps.

For TPS, all $\pm$ values denote cross-seed sample standard deviations over the three \CI{} v15s seeds. The rule oracle in the TPS table is a label-function ceiling for the synthetic policy rules, not a model baseline and not evidence that an LLM has access to the hidden scalar.


\section{Discussion}

\subsection{What the results support}

The results support a causal-channel claim. A continuous elapsed-time scalar can be encoded, injected into the residual stream of a frozen LLM, recovered from hidden states, disabled by zeroing the gate, and perturbed through sign-flip interventions. This is a real mechanistic contribution because it isolates the path by which a non-token scalar affects an otherwise frozen language model.

The strongest evidence is not that the model prints accurate durations. It is that $\Tau$ becomes recoverable immediately after injection, remains recoverable through the network, survives CLOCK-heldout training, and disappears under the appropriate control. That is the evidence that should carry the paper.

\subsection{What the results do not support}

The results do not support claims of subjective time perception. The model does not maintain internal state during silent intervals; it conditions on an externally supplied scalar. The results also do not show that \CI{} is generally better than prompt timestamps. On direct time-recall tasks, prompt injection is simpler and stronger. Finally, T1 and T1b should not be presented as evidence of general time understanding because the same mapping can be approximated by a linear model over the engineered time encoding.

\paragraph{TPS hidden-only test is negative.} The identical-prompt Temporal Policy Switching benchmark (Sec.~VI-G) is the strongest behavioral falsification test in the paper, and \CI{} does not pass it zero-shot. Across three seeds, hidden-only policy accuracy is $0.344\pm0.005$ versus vanilla 0.349, and refresh probability declines slightly with $\log\Tau$ (mean $r=-0.270$). The chrono-only ablation reaches 0.387 on hidden-only items, a modest gain that is not large enough to claim a behavioral contribution. The honest conclusion is that \CI{}, trained on CLOCK + SILENT-GAP + PHASE, transmits $\Tau$ to hidden states (Sec.~VI-A) but does not zero-shot transfer that signal into agent action selection on a cache / session / freshness policy axis. The paper therefore remains a mechanistic side-channel paper with respect to behavioral usefulness: downstream policy use of \CI{} is contingent on policy-specific training, which we leave to future work.

\subsection{Implications}

The main implication is methodological. If an LLM-based system needs elapsed time to affect generation style, decision policy, or internal routing, a continuous residual-stream channel is a plausible design. It also gives researchers a controllable object for mechanistic analysis: the gate can be zeroed, flipped, localized by layer, and compared against prompt-only baselines.

The deployment implication is more cautious. Because $\Tau$ is supplied externally, an application must validate the time source. If the scalar is spoofed, corrupted, or misaligned with the session state, the model may faithfully condition on the wrong time. Chronometric injection should therefore be treated as an input interface, not as a source of temporal truth.

\subsection{Limitations and threats to validity}

The evaluation is narrow. Most results use one base-model family, Qwen 2.5, with three seeds on the 3B model and limited scale checks. The training tasks are synthetic and in-distribution over $[1\ \mathrm{s},7\ \mathrm{days}]$; extrapolation beyond this range fails. Some causal sign-flip results have small parseable effective sample sizes. TPDR and TPS are author-built; TPDR v2 is seed-pair dependent, and TPS is negative for zero-shot policy transfer despite a rule-oracle ceiling.

There are also baseline limitations. Prompt-injected $\Tau$ was tested in multiple formats and is strong on simple recall and on the current \texttt{tau\_sessions} composite, but other alternatives such as learned soft prompts, continuous-value embeddings, prefix tuning, recurrent state, or larger matched adapters remain open. These comparisons are necessary before making strong architectural superiority claims.

A further limitation is reproducibility. The current paper reports the main result families and ablations, but an external submission should include complete training and evaluation details in an appendix or artifact package. In particular, optimizer settings, seeds, hyperparameters, hardware, prompts, dataset-generation scripts, and probe splits should be released or specified.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}

This paper reframes chronometric injection as a causal elapsed-time conditioning channel for a frozen language model. The channel encodes real elapsed seconds, injects the encoding into the residual stream through zero-initialized FiLM gates, and leaves the base model unchanged. The main evidence is mechanistic: the time signal appears in hidden states only when the channel is active, survives removal of clock-readout supervision, collapses under zero-gate controls, and changes under sign-flip interventions.

The defensible contribution is therefore precise: \CI{} shows that continuous elapsed time can be made into a traceable residual-stream input for a frozen LLM. The contribution is not a new FiLM primitive, not a proof of time perception, and not a universal improvement over prompt timestamps. The in-house behavioral tests show that the model can use the channel under trained conditions; the held-out tests reveal which behaviors are format recall; TPDR v2 provides a seed-pair-dependent exploratory response-shape effect; and the completed TPS benchmark is negative for zero-shot policy transfer.

Future work should test the channel on more base-model families, larger models, external non-synthetic tasks, and stronger prompt or soft-prompt baselines. A second direction is to combine elapsed-time conditioning with persistent memory or recurrent state so that the model can reason over both externally supplied wall-clock time and internally stored session history. A third direction is to train and evaluate policy-specific elapsed-time tasks directly, because the completed TPS test shows that the current CLOCK + SILENT-GAP + PHASE training mix does not yield zero-shot policy use of the hidden scalar.
